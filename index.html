The Short Version

In one paragraph: A coin WiFi machine is a small router with a billing brain. It gives your phone a private address, sets itself (often 10.0.0.1) as your gateway, blocks the internet until you have credit, and keeps your remaining time or data tied to your device. Pausing simply tells the machine to stop deducting from that stored balance until you ask it to continue.

If you only remember three things, make them these: the gateway address is the doorway to the portal, your device identity is the key to your balance, and the owner's settings decide which features, including pause, you actually get.

Why Vendos Love 10.0.0.1

Every home or shop network uses private addresses that never travel across the public internet. Three blocks are reserved for this purpose, and the 10.0.0.0 block is the largest. A vendo does not need a public address for its portal; it only needs a simple, memorable one that customers can type if the automatic pop-up fails.

Private blockRangeTypical use
10.0.0.0/810.0.0.0 to 10.255.255.255Large or custom networks; many vendo portals
172.16.0.0/12172.16.0.0 to 172.31.255.255Medium networks, some corporate setups
192.168.0.0/16192.168.0.0 to 192.168.255.255Typical home routers

The tidy, round-looking 10.0.0.1 is simply the first usable address in its block. Firmware authors like it because it is short, and because it rarely clashes with the 192.168.x.x addresses used by most upstream home modems. Avoiding that clash matters: if the vendo and the modem behind it share the same subnet, routing breaks in confusing ways.

The "10.0.0.0.1" Typo Problem

Search logs are full of people looking for 10.0.0.0.1, and it is easy to see why: the address is often read aloud or copied from a screenshot, and an extra zero slips in. The trouble is that an IPv4 address has exactly four numbers separated by dots, each between 0 and 255. Five numbers is not an address at all, so your browser treats it as a search term or throws an error.

What to do instead: type exactly 10.0.0.1, with no extra characters, no "www", and no secure prefix at first. Use http://10.0.0.1. Many local portals do not have a certificate, so forcing https:// can show a warning or fail outright.

Try the IP checker further down this page; it flags five-part addresses and tells you whether an address belongs to a private range.

How the Pieces Connect

Traffic from your phone goes to the access point, then to the vendo brain, which decides whether you may pass to the modem and out to the internet. The coin acceptor talks only to the brain. That is why a dead coin acceptor does not necessarily take down the Wi-Fi, and why a misbehaving modem can leave you "connected" yet unable to load anything.

DHCP: How Your Phone Gets Its Address

When you join the network, your phone broadcasts a request. The vendo's DHCP service answers with four pieces of information: an address for you (for example 10.0.0.37), the subnet mask, the gateway (10.0.0.1), and a DNS server. Without that exchange, you would show "connected" but have no usable settings.

Why leases matter

Addresses are loaned for a limited time. If a vendo has a short lease and many customers, addresses recycle quickly. A device that returns after a long pause may receive a different address than before. This is normal and harmless, as long as the machine tracks you by device identity rather than by the address itself.

Subnet capacity at a glance

In practice the radio is the bottleneck long before addresses run out. A single access point rarely serves comfortably more than a few dozen active phones at once.

Finding Your Gateway Address

Instead of guessing, read the address your device was given. The "gateway" or "router" entry is the one you type to reach the portal.

Android

Wi-Fi settings, tap the connected network, then open its details or advanced section and look for Gateway or Router.

iPhone

Settings, Wi-Fi, tap the info icon beside the network, then read the Router field.

Windows

Open Command Prompt, run ipconfig, and read the Default Gateway line for your Wi-Fi adapter.

macOS

System Settings, Network, choose Wi-Fi, open Details, and read the Router field under TCP/IP.

If the gateway shows something other than 10.0.0.1, use that value. Machines differ, and an owner may have changed the default.

Captive Portals: The Pop-Up That Isn't Always There

When you join a Wi-Fi network, modern phones quietly request a known test page to see whether the internet works. If the vendo intercepts that request and returns its own page instead, the phone concludes it is behind a captive portal and prompts you to sign in. That prompt is automatic on many devices, but not all, and not always.

Things that stop the pop-up

  • Mobile data left on. Some phones prefer the connection that appears to have internet and skip the Wi-Fi prompt.
  • Private DNS or encrypted DNS. The phone's test request goes to a different resolver, so the vendo never gets to intercept it.
  • VPN apps. Traffic is tunneled before the vendo can redirect it.
  • Cached pages. An old portal tab may refer to an expired session.
Reliable fallback: disable mobile data and any VPN, switch Private DNS off, then open http://10.0.0.1 directly in your browser. Typing the address bypasses the whole detection step.

Device Identity and MAC Addresses

A vendo cannot trust a changing IP address to know who you are, so it usually keys your balance to your device's hardware identifier, the MAC address. Modern phones can present a random or "private" MAC address per network to protect privacy, which is good for tracking protection but awkward for coin WiFi.

SettingEffect on a vendoRecommendation
Fixed (device) MACMachine recognises you every timeBest for pause and resume
Random MAC, stable per networkUsually recognised on the same networkOften fine
Random MAC, rotatingMachine may see you as a new deviceTurn off for vendo networks

If your balance "disappears" after a pause, an identity change is the first thing to rule out, long before blaming the machine.

Pause Time Under the Hood

Understanding the mechanism makes the rules feel logical. Here is what the machine is doing when you use piso wifi pause on a time-based vendo.

  1. You press Pause. The portal sends a small request to the vendo's billing service, identifying you by device identity.
  2. The balance is frozen. The service records your remaining seconds and marks the session as paused rather than active.
  3. Network access is cut. Your device is moved to the "no credit" state, so the firewall blocks internet traffic again.
  4. A timer may start. If the owner set a maximum pause window, a separate countdown begins.
  5. You press Resume. The service looks up your frozen balance, reopens access, and restarts the deduction clock.

The saved balance lives on the vendo, not on your phone and not on a remote server. That is why a paused balance is useless on a different machine, and why a factory reset or a replaced memory card can wipe it.

Edge cases worth knowing

Power cut during a pause

Well-built firmware writes balances to storage, so they survive. Cheap builds may keep them only in memory.

Owner changes settings

Lowering the maximum pause window can affect sessions already paused.

Voucher with pause

Voucher time usually joins the same balance, though some machines treat it separately.

Roaming between access points

If two radios share one brain, you keep your balance. If they are separate machines, you do not.

Admin Access and Hardening for Owners

The management page usually lives on the same address as the portal, under an admin path. Reach it only from a device on your own vendo network. The point of a private address is that the admin page should never be visible from the public internet.

First-day checklist

  1. Replace the default login. Factory credentials are widely known. Change both the username and the password the day you install.
  2. Use a long unique password. A passphrase beats a short complex one, and it must not be reused elsewhere.
  3. Check what is exposed. Make sure remote management or port forwarding is off unless you truly need it.
  4. Update firmware. Use only files from the maker or a source you trust; unofficial builds are a security gamble.
  5. Back up the configuration. Export it so a reset does not cost you your prices, vouchers and pause rules.
  6. Test as a customer. Use a spare phone to confirm Insert Money, Pause and Resume behave as intended.
Why bother? A compromised vendo can leak customer traffic, hand free time to strangers, or become part of someone else's attack. A ten-minute hardening pass removes most of that risk.

A Troubleshooting Flow That Actually Works

Work from the bottom of the stack upward. Each step rules out one layer.

StepCheckIf it fails
1. RadioIs the vendo network name visible with decent signal?Move closer; ask the owner about the access point.
2. AssociationDoes your phone say Connected, not "Saved" or "Authentication problem"?Forget the network and rejoin.
3. AddressDo Wi-Fi details show an IP like 10.0.0.x and a gateway?Toggle Wi-Fi; the DHCP service may be stuck.
4. PortalDoes http://10.0.0.1 load?Disable mobile data, VPN and Private DNS; try another browser.
5. CreditDoes the portal show remaining time or data?Insert coins and press Done Paying; check pause state.
6. UpstreamDo sites load, or only the portal?The modem or ISP line is down; contact the owner.
7. SpeedIs it slow only at certain hours?Congestion or a speed cap; try off-peak.

Speed, Fairness and Why It Slows Down

One shared line has to serve everyone. If an owner pays for a modest plan and a handful of people stream video, everyone feels it. Owners can limit damage with per-user caps; users can help by trimming background traffic.

  • Airtime is shared. More phones means each gets a smaller slice of the same radio channel.
  • Distance and walls matter. A weak signal forces the phone to use slower modulation, which also slows other users.
  • Uploads count too. Photo backups can saturate a small upstream link.
  • Peak hours are real. After school and early evening are typically busiest.
Owner tip: a modest, steady per-user cap often produces happier customers than raw maximum speed, because pages load consistently instead of swinging between fast and stalled.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 10.0.0.0.1 a valid IP address?

No. IPv4 addresses have exactly four numbers. Five-part entries are almost always typos of 10.0.0.1.

Why is 10.0.0.1 so common on vendos?

It is a short, memorable address in a private range, and it seldom collides with the 192.168.x.x network of a typical upstream modem.

I typed the address but the page is blank. Why?

Check for a VPN, mobile data or Private DNS. Also confirm you are on the vendo network and that you typed http, not https.

Where is my paused balance stored?

On the vendo itself, linked to your device identity. It does not follow you to another machine.

Why did my balance vanish after pausing?

Common causes are a changed or random MAC address, a pause window that expired, or the owner resetting the machine.

Can two phones share one session?

Generally no. Sessions are tied to individual devices.

Is the admin page reachable from home?

It should not be. A private address is only reachable from inside the local network unless the owner has opened remote access.

Does a longer pause cost me anything?

Normally not, but the owner may clear balances after a set window.

How many users can one vendo handle?

The address pool is rarely the limit. Radio capacity and the internet plan are the real ceilings.

Should owners use the default gateway address?

It is fine, but make sure it does not overlap with the upstream modem's subnet, and always change default admin credentials.
